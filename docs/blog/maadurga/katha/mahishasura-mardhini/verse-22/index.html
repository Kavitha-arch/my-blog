Mahishasura Mardhini

ॐ

Dṛg-Dṛśya Viveka


Śiva (Sākṣī) without Durgā (Śakti) is inert; Śakti without Śiva is directionless.

******Dakṣiṇāmūrti Stotram*******

विश्वं दर्पणदृश्यमाननगरीतुल्यं निजान्तर्गतम् ।

******** BG 2.48****************

योगस्थः कुरु कर्माणि सङ्गं त्यक्त्वा धनञ्जय ।

सिद्ध्यसिद्ध्योः समो भूत्वा समत्वं योग उच्यते ॥ २.४८ ॥

१. The entire universe exists within oneself, like a city reflected in a mirror.

Śiva as Pure Consciousness (Sākṣī) illumines all experiences while remaining untouched.

The movements of the mind are occurring in awareness. Thoughts, emotions, memories, and reactions are seen. Therefore, they are not the ultimate seer.

२. BG 2.48 Be established in Sākṣī; let Śakti act with equanimity.

Established in yoga, perform your actions, abandoning attachment, O Dhanañjaya. Remaining even-minded in success and failure—this equanimity is called Yoga.

Salutations to the Goddess who abides in all beings as buddhi (intelligence/discernment).


Story

  • Life consists of changing situations, events, relationships, opportunities, and challenges. It is not designed to provide uninterrupted comfort or happiness.
  • A Seeker: who wishes to live with greater peace—can cultivate the habit of remembering the Sākṣī, the witnessing awareness, not only during crises but also during ordinary moments throughout the day. Regularly recognizing 'I am aware of this experience' makes it easier to remain steady when difficulties arise.
  • The Role of the Sākṣī: is not to force acceptance or suppress emotions. Rather, it creates a space between the experience and the reaction. In that space, the mind can move from worry, anger, fear, or sadness toward clearer understanding and wiser action. Sometimes the situation can be changed; sometimes it cannot. In either case, one is less likely to be completely carried away by the mind's immediate reactions.
  • Note: This approach may not eliminate every problem, but it can foster a deeper sense of inner stability and peace, independent of changing circumstances.
  • Afterward, it is also valuable to reflect: 'I noticed my reaction. I paused. I responded more wisely than I might have before.' From a psychological perspective, consciously acknowledging such progress reinforces helpful mental habits and makes them more likely to recur. From a Vedāntic perspective, it also strengthens discrimination (viveka) and reduces habitual identification with the mind.

✍️ रक्ष रक्ष जगन्माते सर्वशक्ते जय दुर्गे।🙏

Life is a continuous flow of situations, relationships, events, and challenges—not a journey of uninterrupted comfort. My body and mind, through the dynamic power of Śakti, will naturally react with pleasure, pain, likes, dislikes, worry, or joy. My practice is to repeatedly remember that I am the Sākṣī, the witnessing awareness and the stillness of Śiva, that knows these experiences but is not limited by them. Recognizing the witness does not mean suppressing emotions or passively accepting everything; it creates the inner space needed for the mind to move from reaction to wise response and practical action. This may not remove every problem, but it helps me remain balanced in the movement of Śakti while rooted in the peace of Śiva. Each time I notice a reaction, pause, and respond with greater clarity, I strengthen this awareness, gradually weakening old patterns and cultivating inner freedom, steadiness, and wise living.

Rewarding the Brain

Neuroscience suggests that reflecting on successful coping can reinforce adaptive neural pathways. Vedānta would frame the same process differently: repeated recognition of the witness and repeated wise responses weaken habitual reactions (vāsanās) and strengthen steadiness (samatva). The two perspectives are compatible—they simply describe the process at different levels. One speaks in terms of brain function and learning; the other in terms of the mind's conditioning and Self-knowledge.
